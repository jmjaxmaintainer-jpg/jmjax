\section{Blocked samplers and the association parameter}\label{sec:blocked}

The location degeneracy does not involve $\alpha$. Yet on $\alpha$,
\texttt{jmjax}'s NUTS was 3--8 times more efficient per second than
JMbayes2 even without the rotation: 5.7--8.1 times across a replicated
simulation sweep with 1 to 12 covariates (each package's own ESS
estimator), and 3.5 and 8.2 times on the two datasets of
Section~\ref{sec:realdata} (one estimator for both). JMbayes2 updates the
parameters in blocks, with adaptive random-walk Metropolis steps for the
coefficients. We argue that, on the two datasets measured, about three
quarters (on a log scale) of the ESS-per-draw gap on $\alpha$ is what even
an ideal two-block sampler would incur.

Write $\psi$ for every parameter other than $\alpha$: the random effects,
$\beta$, $\gamma$, the baseline-hazard coefficients and the variance
components.

\begin{proposition}[Blocked updates of $\alpha$]\label{prop:blocked}
Consider the two-block sampler that draws $\alpha$ exactly from
$p(\alpha \mid \psi, y)$ and then $\psi$ exactly from $p(\psi \mid
\alpha, y)$, and let
\[
r = \frac{\Var\{\E(\alpha \mid \psi, y)\}}{\Var(\alpha \mid y)}
= 1 - \frac{\E\{\Var(\alpha \mid \psi, y)\}}{\Var(\alpha \mid y)},
\]
the fraction of $\alpha$'s posterior variance explained by the other
parameters, and $R^2 \leq r$ the squared multiple correlation of $\alpha$
on $\psi$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] At stationarity the lag-1 autocorrelation of the $\alpha$ chain
is $r$.
\item[(ii)] Its integrated autocorrelation time is at least $(1 + r)/(1 -
r)$, so its effective sample size per draw is at most $(1 - r)/(1 + r)
\leq (1 - R^2)/(1 + R^2)$.
\item[(iii)] If the posterior is Gaussian, $r = R^2$, the $\alpha$ chain
is AR(1) with coefficient $R^2$, and the bound in (ii) is attained.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The $\alpha$ chain of a two-block sampler is a data-augmentation chain
\citep{liu1994}. Let $P$ map a function $h$ of $\alpha$ to the function
$(Ph)(\psi) = \E\{h(\alpha) \mid \psi, y\}$, and $P^{*}$ map $g$ to
$(P^{*}g)(\alpha) = \E\{g(\psi) \mid \alpha, y\}$; these are adjoint in
the $L^2$ inner products of the posterior. The transition operator of the
$\alpha$ chain is $K = P^{*}P$, so $K$ is self-adjoint and $\langle Kh, h
\rangle = \|Ph\|^2 \geq 0$. With $h(\alpha) = \alpha - \E(\alpha \mid y)$,
the lag-1 autocovariance is $\langle Kh, h \rangle = \Var\{\E(\alpha \mid
\psi, y)\}$, which gives (i); the second
expression for $r$ is the law of total variance, and $R^2 \leq r$ because
the best linear predictor of $\alpha$ explains no more variance than
$\E(\alpha \mid \psi, y)$. For (ii), the spectral theorem writes the
lag-$k$ autocorrelation of $\alpha$ as $\rho_k = \int \lambda^k\,
\mu(d\lambda)$ for a probability measure $\mu$ on $[0, 1]$, so Jensen's
inequality gives $\rho_k \geq \rho_1^k = r^k$ and $1 + 2\sum_{k \geq 1}
\rho_k \geq (1 + r)/(1 - r)$; the function $(1 - x)/(1 + x)$ is
decreasing. For (iii), $\E(\alpha \mid \psi)$ is linear in $\psi$ and
$\E(\psi \mid \alpha)$ linear in $\alpha$; composing the two regressions
gives $\alpha_{t+1} = c + R^2 \alpha_t + \varepsilon_t$ with
$\varepsilon_t$ independent of the past, since the coefficient is
$\operatorname{Cov}(\alpha, \psi)\,\Sigma_\psi^{-1}
\operatorname{Cov}(\psi, \alpha) / \Var(\alpha) = R^2$, and then
$\rho_k = (R^2)^k$.
\end{proof}

Part (ii) makes a measured linear $R^2$ usable: it bounds the
efficiency of any exact two-block sampler from above, whatever the shape
of the posterior. The proposition applies known theory; the two-block
sampler is a benchmark, not a model of JMbayes2, and finer blocking and
random-walk Metropolis steps can be expected to make a real implementation
slower still \citep{liu1994,roberts1997}, though not in every case. The
contrast with HMC, which moves $\alpha$ and $\psi$ together, is a
heuristic. Under a fixed metric a correlation between them is paid for in
leapfrog steps, which for a Gaussian target grow with the square root of
the condition number \citep{neal2011}. A single correlation at level $R^2$
contributes a condition number $(1 + \sqrt{R^2})/(1 - \sqrt{R^2})$, so the
cost grows at most like $1/\sqrt{1 - R^2}$, against $1/(1 - R^2)$ for the
blocked sampler, and often not at all, because in a joint model the step
size is already set by some other narrow direction.
\suppref{sec:gausscheck} checks both statements in a Gaussian model.

\subsection{How large $R^2$ is in a joint model}

Two mechanisms can make $R^2$ large. The first is errors in variables:
given the random effects, $\alpha$ is the coefficient of a known
time-varying covariate, while marginally it also carries the uncertainty
in every trajectory $m_i(t)$. The second is the level of the hazard: a
common shift of every $m_i(t)$ changes $\alpha\, m_i(t)$ by an amount the
log baseline hazard absorbs, so $\alpha$ is correlated with the
baseline-hazard level unless the marker is centered.

We estimated the linear explained variance $R^2$ by an ordinary
least-squares regression of the 20,000 draws of $\alpha$ from a long
rotated fit (4 chains of 5000 draws, close to independent) on every other
sampled quantity (960 regressors for \texttt{aids} and 649 for
\texttt{pbc2}), and report the adjusted $R^2$, which differs from the
unadjusted value by less than 0.01. We also regressed $\alpha$ on each block
alone. On \texttt{aids}, $R^2 = 0.873$; the survival block
alone ($\gamma$ and the baseline-hazard coefficients) explained 0.86 of
$\alpha$'s posterior variance and the random effects alone 0.30. On
\texttt{pbc2} the figures were 0.853, 0.82 and 0.20: in these two
datasets, most of the explained variance is attributable to the survival
block rather than the random effects. By Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked},
$R^2 \leq r$, so
the efficiency it implies for an ideal blocked sampler is an upper bound.

Figure~\ref{fig:alpha} puts the measurements against the prediction. At
the measured $R^2$ an ideal two-block sampler keeps at most 0.068
(\texttt{aids}) and 0.079 (\texttt{pbc2}) effective draws per draw, one
in 13--15. JMbayes2 achieved 0.032 and 0.025 (its draws from the runs of
Section~\ref{sec:realdata}, NumPyro's estimator; its own gives 0.035 and 0.028),
and \texttt{jmjax}'s NUTS 1.05 and 1.72 in the long fit. On a log scale, the ideal benchmark accounts for 78\% of the measured
ESS-per-draw gap on \texttt{aids} (15.4 of 33 times) and 73\% on
\texttt{pbc2} (21.7 of 68 times); the rest, a factor of two to
three, is attributable to the implementation. A blocked sampler could
escape most of the structural part by updating $\alpha$ jointly with
$\gamma$ and the baseline-hazard coefficients: with only the random
effects conditioned away, the ideal ESS per draw would be about 0.54 and
0.67. But that block has 10--20 strongly correlated dimensions without
exact conditional draws, and an adaptive random-walk proposal on it is
itself slow. HMC moves $\alpha$ and $\psi$ jointly without a designed
block, although how well it does so still depends on the metric and on
the rest of the geometry.
