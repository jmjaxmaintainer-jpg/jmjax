\section{Beyond joint models}\label{sec:generality}

Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:absorbable}--\ref{sec:rotation} depends on
the survival submodel except Condition~\ref{cond:S}. To check that the
gains are not specific to joint models, we applied the construction, in a
standalone NumPyro program, to two classical mixed models. For
\texttt{Orthodont} \citep{potthoff1964,pinheiro2000}, 27 children
measured at 4 ages, a Gaussian linear mixed model (LMM) $\text{distance} \sim \text{age}
\times \text{male} + (1 + \text{age} \mid \text{child})$ with age
centered has absorbable directions $\{\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{male}\}$ in both
random-effect columns ($k = 2$). For the toenail onychomycosis trial
\citep{debacker1998,lesaffre2001}, 1908 binary outcomes on 294 patients,
a logistic generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) $y \sim \text{trt} \times \text{time} + (1 \mid
\text{patient})$ has absorbable directions $\{\mathbf{1},
\mathrm{trt}\}$. Both use the non-centered parameterization, as brms and rstanarm do by
default, with $\N(0, 10^2)$ priors on $\beta$, half-normal(5) priors on
the standard deviations and an LKJ(2) prior on the correlation. Five arms
were run on each (4 chains of 1000 warm-up and 1000 sampling iterations, 3
seeds): \emph{unrotated} with a diagonal metric (the reference);
\emph{rotated, diagonal}, the rotation alone; \emph{rotated, dense
block}, the method; \emph{unrotated, full dense}, a dense metric over
every sampled coordinate, the obvious alternative way to learn the ridge;
and \emph{centered}, sampling $b_i \sim \N(0, LL^{\top})$ with a diagonal
metric, the classical remedy \citep{gelfand1995}. Because each run takes
seconds, we report ESS per 1000 gradient evaluations, which does not
depend on the machine, and ESS per second of the whole run as the
practical measure.

With the method, the intercept and the subject-constant coefficients
gained 5.5--7.1 times per gradient and 4.2--5.1 times per second, and the
coefficients absorbed by the random slope in \texttt{Orthodont} 2.4--2.5
times per gradient (Table~\ref{tab:gen}). The rotation alone supplied
2.3--2.8 on the intercept and subject-constant coefficients and nothing on
the slope-absorbed ones; the dense block supplied the rest, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:ridge} predicts. The coefficients with no
absorbable direction (toenail's time and treatment-by-time), a negative
control, moved by 1.3--1.4 per gradient and 1.0 per second.

\paragraph{Why not a full dense metric?} In \texttt{Orthodont}, with
$N = 27$ and 62 sampled coordinates, a full dense metric recovered
most of the gain per gradient (5.5--5.6 against 7.1 for the intercept and
male) and did somewhat better on the variance components, but at twice the
cost per run, so per second it gained 2.0--2.1 against the method's 4.7.
In the toenail model, with $N = 294$ and about 300 coordinates, it was worse than the method on every measure: a 300-dimensional metric estimated from 1000
warm-up iterations needed 47.6 leapfrog steps per draw against 21.2, per
gradient it gained 3.2--3.7 against 5.5--6.7, and each run took 167
seconds against 7, so per second it was 0.2--0.3 times the \emph{unrotated}
fit. This is a result for the tested adaptation budget, not a limit of
dense NUTS: a longer warm-up would estimate the metric better, at a cost
that grows with the number of coordinates. The rotation makes a dense
metric affordable by moving the ridge into $p + kq$ coordinates (8 and 6
here), whatever $N$ is.

\paragraph{Why not center?} In these two configurations centering did
not remove the degeneracy's cost (per gradient 0.4 on the
\texttt{Orthodont} intercept and male coefficients, 0.8--1.1 on
toenail's) and brought back the funnel: in \texttt{Orthodont} it produced
34 divergent transitions over the 3 seeds, $\hat R$ up to 1.047 and 52.5
leapfrog steps per draw, and the slope standard deviation mixed at 0.06
times the unrotated rate; in toenail the random-effect standard deviation
mixed at 0.35 times. Both models carry little information per subject,
the regime in which centering is known to do poorly
\citep{gelfand1995,browne2009}; where subjects are informative, centering
can work well, as JMbayes2's centered coefficients do in
Section~\ref{sec:realdata}. The rotation needs no choice between the two
and, in every design tested, reduced the location cost within the
non-centered parameterization.

Across all arms, posterior means agreed with the unrotated fit's to within
0.14 posterior standard deviations. Apart from the centered arm, $\hat R
\leq 1.011$ and there were at most 2 divergences (full dense, \texttt{Orthodont}).
