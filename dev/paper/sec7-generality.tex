\section{Beyond joint models}\label{sec:generality}

Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:absorbable}--\ref{sec:rotation} depends on
the survival submodel except Condition~\ref{cond:S}. To check that the
gains are not specific to joint models, we applied the construction, in a
standalone NumPyro program that does not use \texttt{jmjax}, to two
classical mixed models, each with a subject-constant covariate:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{Orthodont} \citep{potthoff1964,pinheiro2000}: a Gaussian
linear mixed model for the distance measure of 27 children at 4 ages,
$\text{distance} \sim \text{age} \times \text{male} + (1 + \text{age} \mid
\text{child})$ with age centred. Sex is subject-constant, so both
random-effect columns have absorbable directions $\{\mathbf{1},
\mathrm{male}\}$, $k = 2$.
\item The toenail onychomycosis trial \citep{debacker1998,lesaffre2001}: a
logistic mixed model for 1908 binary outcomes on 294 patients, $y \sim
\text{trt} \times \text{time} + (1 \mid \text{patient})$. Treatment is
subject-constant, so the absorbable directions are $\{\mathbf{1},
\mathrm{trt}\}$.
\end{itemize}
Both use the non-centred parameterization with $\N(0, 10^2)$ priors on
$\beta$, half-normal(5) priors on the standard deviations and an LKJ(2)
prior on the correlation, which is the parameterization brms and rstanarm
use by default. Five arms were run on each, with 4 chains of 1000 warm-up
and 1000 sampling iterations and 3 seeds, all in the same program on the
same data:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{unrotated}, diagonal metric (the reference);
\item \emph{rotated, diagonal}: the rotation alone;
\item \emph{rotated, dense block}: the method, a dense metric on $(\beta,
U)$ only;
\item \emph{unrotated, full dense}: no rotation, and a dense metric over
every sampled coordinate, the obvious alternative way to let the sampler
learn the ridge;
\item \emph{centred}: the random effects themselves are sampled, $b_i \sim
\N(0, LL^{\top})$, with a diagonal metric, the classical remedy for the
location degeneracy \citep{gelfand1995}.
\end{itemize}
Because each run takes seconds, we report ESS per 1000 gradient
evaluations, which does not depend on the machine, and ESS per second of
the whole run (warm-up and compilation included) as the practical measure.

Table~\ref{tab:gen} gives the results. With the method, the intercept and
the subject-constant coefficients gained 5.5--7.1 times per gradient and
4.2--5.1 times per second, and the coefficients absorbed by the random
slope in \texttt{Orthodont} 2.4--2.5 times per gradient. The rotation with
a diagonal metric alone supplied a factor of 2.3--2.8 on the intercept and
subject-constant coefficients and nothing on the slope-absorbed ones; the
dense block supplied the rest, as Proposition~\ref{prop:ridge} predicts.
The coefficients with no absorbable direction (toenail's time and
treatment-by-time) moved by 1.3--1.4 per gradient and 1.0 per second, a
negative control.

\paragraph{Why not a full dense metric?} Letting NUTS adapt a dense metric
over every coordinate, without the rotation, is the direct alternative. In
\texttt{Orthodont}, with $N = 27$ and 62 sampled coordinates, it recovered
most of the gain per gradient (5.5--5.6 against 7.1 for the intercept and
male) and did somewhat better on the variance components, but at twice the
cost per run, so per second it gained 2.0--2.1 against the method's 4.7.
In the toenail model, with $N = 294$ and about 300 coordinates, it was worse than the method on every measure: a 300-dimensional metric estimated from 1000
warm-up iterations needed 47.6 leapfrog steps per draw against 21.2, per
gradient it gained 3.2--3.7 against 5.5--6.7, and each run took 167
seconds against 7, so per second it was 0.2--0.3 times the \emph{unrotated}
fit. This is a result for the tested adaptation budget, not a limit of dense
NUTS as such: a longer warm-up would estimate the metric better, at a
cost that grows with the number of coordinates. The rotation is what makes a
dense metric affordable: it moves the ridge into $p + kq$ coordinates (8 and
6 here), whatever $N$ is.

\paragraph{Why not centre?} In these two configurations the centred
parameterization did not remove the degeneracy's cost: per gradient it gave 0.4 on the
\texttt{Orthodont} intercept and male coefficients and 0.8--1.1 on toenail's.
It also brought back the funnel that non-centring avoids. In
\texttt{Orthodont}, with 4 observations per child, it produced 34
divergent transitions over the 3 seeds, $\hat R$ up to 1.047 and 52.5
leapfrog steps per draw, and the slope standard deviation mixed at 0.06
times the unrotated rate; in toenail, with binary outcomes, the random-effect
standard deviation mixed at 0.35 times. Both carry little information per
subject, the regime in which centring is known to do poorly
\citep{gelfand1995,browne2009}; where subjects are informative, centring
can work well, as JMbayes2's centred coefficients do on the joint-model data
of Section~\ref{sec:realdata}. The rotation needs no choice between the two
parameterizations: it keeps the non-centred one and removes the location
cost within it, whatever the information per subject.

Across all arms, posterior means agreed with the unrotated fit's to within
0.14 posterior standard deviations. Apart from the centred arm, $\hat R
\leq 1.011$ and there were at most 2 divergences (full dense, \texttt{Orthodont}).
