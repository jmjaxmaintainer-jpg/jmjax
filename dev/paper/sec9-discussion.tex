\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Choosing a parameterization.} That the parameterization of a
hierarchical model decides how fast a sampler mixes is well established.
Hierarchical centring \citep{gelfand1995,gelfand1996} removes the location
degeneracy by sampling the centred effects; the non-centred
parameterization removes the funnel instead
\citep{papaspiliopoulos2003,papaspiliopoulos2007}; partial non-centring
interpolates, and ASIS \citep{yu2011} interweaves the two within a Gibbs
sampler. \citet{gorinova2020} automate the choice per variable in
probabilistic programs. All of these choose between centred and
non-centred coordinates for each random effect. The rotation does not: it
keeps the non-centred parameterization, with its benefits for the
variances, and changes only which linear combinations of the standardized
effects are sampling coordinates. In the comparison with JMbayes2, which
centres the coefficients it can, this recovered what centring buys on
those coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}). \citet{browne2009}
studied three reparameterizations for multilevel survival models under
Gibbs sampling. Their orthogonal parameterization orthogonalizes the
fixed-effect predictors among themselves, as Stan's QR reparameterization
does \citep{stanug}, but they observe that predictors correlated with the
cluster indicator vectors make fixed and random effects ``play similar
roles'' and suggest orthogonality to those indicators as future work. The
absorbable subspace is that span, handled by rotating the random effects
rather than the predictors.

\paragraph{Removing the directions.} A second family removes the
absorbable directions. Sum-to-zero constraints are the classical example.
\citet{zanella2021} show that imposing such constraints always improves
the Gibbs sampler for crossed random effects, and that it also helps NUTS
dramatically on a Poisson crossed model (minimum ESS from 3.7 to 4977).
Their construction samples the posterior conditioned on the constraint,
and Stan's \texttt{sum\_to\_zero\_vector} likewise changes the prior unless
compensated. Restricted spatial regression \citep{reich2006,hughes2013}
projects spatial random effects off the fixed-effect design, and is known
to affect inference on the fixed effects
\citep{hodges2010,hanks2015,khan2022}. An earlier version of
\texttt{jmjax} did the same for the absorbable directions: it swept them
out of the sampled random-effect coordinates. Without a correction this
miscalibrated the intervals for the swept coefficients; with an exact
correction it was about as efficient as the rotation in ordinary designs
and up to twice as efficient in the most information-dense ones
(Section~\ref{sec:stress}), at the price of the correction step. The
closest precedent is \citet{vines1996}, whose transformation is exact: for
independent Gaussian random effects and a Gaussian prior on the grand mean,
the directions that shift the grand mean against the mean of each factor
are a priori independent of everything else, appear in no likelihood term,
and can be integrated out, leaving the grand mean plus the mean effects and
sum-to-zero contrasts to sample. Their method covers the grand-mean
direction only, reports contrasts rather than the original parameters, and
leaves correlated random effects to further work; they also warn that the
\emph{ad hoc} alternative, recentring the effects inside each iteration,
has an unknown effect on the stationary distribution. The rotation
extends the exact approach to every absorbable direction, including the
covariate-weighted ones and those of random slopes, to correlated random
effects and to any prior on the fixed effects, and it returns the original
parameters.

\paragraph{Analysing the directions.} In the discussion of
\citet{zanella2021}, \citet{yang2021} decompose the Gibbs sampler for a
random-intercept model with covariates using the subject-level covariate
means, which is the covariate-weighted absorbable subspace for the
intercept column, and show that the convergence rate is governed by the
correlation between $\beta$ and the random effects along it. They use the
decomposition to analyse Gibbs samplers, note that it breaks down for
general designs, and do not treat random slopes; here the subspace is
constructed exactly for every column from the design and used as sampling
coordinates. In the same discussion \citet{robert2021} asked whether the
effect of these parameterization choices had been studied for HMC, and the
rejoinder \citep{zanella2021} called that open. Lemma~\ref{lem:local} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:ridge} are an analysis on the HMC side for the
absorbable directions: what a diagonal metric can and cannot do, what a
dense block adds, and where a fixed metric falls short. They complement
general results on linear preconditioning for MCMC
\citep{hirdlivingstone2025}: the block the rotation creates is small enough
to precondition densely because the model says where the ridge is.

\paragraph{Joint-model software.} JMbayes2 \citep{jmbayes2} uses
Metropolis-within-Gibbs; the function \texttt{stan\_jm} in the rstanarm package
\citep{brilleman2018} uses NUTS with non-centred random effects and so, we
expect, meets the same ridge, which we have not tested; INLAjoint
\citep{rustand2024} uses integrated nested Laplace approximations and does
not sample. To our knowledge, the application of the data-augmentation
autocorrelation identity \citep{liu1994} to the association parameter,
together with the measurement that places most of the shared variance in
the baseline hazard, has not been reported before.

\subsection{Costs and limits}

Two costs were measured and left as they are. With about 9 visits per
subject, the rotation gave $\alpha$ 0.72--1.00 of the unrotated fit's ESS
per second, though it gained with denser follow-up and $\alpha$ remained
far ahead of the blocked sampler either way; the mechanism is not
established. And in the most information-dense stress designs, the swept
variant was up to twice as efficient, as the fixed-metric residual of
Section~\ref{sec:residual} predicts, although the rotation was still
5--30 times ahead of the unrotated fit there. The simulation evidence comes from one design family with a
spline baseline hazard. The basis construction's rank decision is a
tolerance judgement when absorbable and identified directions are nearly
collinear, although every returned direction is verified before use.
Condition~\ref{cond:S} is checked at each fit rather than assumed, and can
fail for very flexible time bases with few observations per subject.

\subsection{Extensions}

A metric that follows the sampled $\sigma_0$, for example by rescaling the
dense block with the current value of $L$, is the obvious way to remove
the residual of Section~\ref{sec:residual}, and has not been tried. The
construction needs only the fixed- and random-effects designs, so it
applies to any model whose random effects enter through a linear
predictor, including the GLMMs of brms and rstanarm, and could be offered
there as a transformation of the non-centred random effects. Crossed
random effects need one basis per grouping factor, each rotating that
factor's own index; the argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} carries
over unchanged.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The location degeneracy between fixed and random effects is shared by
every hierarchical regression with subject-constant covariates, and it
recurs for the time slope in every model with a random slope. It slows HMC
on the coefficients an analyst reports. Rotating the standardized random
effects so that the absorbable directions become a small block of
coordinates, and giving that block and the fixed effects one dense metric
block, removes it without changing the model: in simulation by factors of
14 and 21 for the intercept and time slope, on real joint-model data by
17--28 on the intercept and subject-constant covariates, and on the same
coefficients by 5--7 per gradient in two ordinary mixed models. The association parameter of a
joint model is a separate matter. Its posterior variance is largely shared
with the level of the baseline hazard, and a sampler that updates it
conditionally pays for that sharing in autocorrelation, whereas HMC pays,
if at all, in integration steps.
